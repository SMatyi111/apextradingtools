Pip and contract conventions used here

Wrong pip sizes are the most common reason lot size calculators and journals give wrong answers (a JPY pair treated like EURUSD is off by a factor of 100). These are the values this page uses. If your broker differs, use the entry and stop prices instead of pips, and set the contract size under Broker settings.

Gold has no single "pip" convention: some traders and sites call 0.01 a pip, others 0.10 or 1.00. Here 1 pip = 0.10, so a move from 2000.00 to 1990.00 is 100 pips. Entering the entry and stop price avoids the question entirely. Values per pip are converted to your account currency with the ECB reference rate of the day, or the rate you type. When the account currency is the pair's base currency (USDJPY on a USD account) and you enter prices, the conversion uses your entry price.

FAQ

How is the lot size calculated?

Lot size = money at risk ÷ (stop distance in price × contract size × exchange rate from the quote currency to your account currency), rounded down to your broker's lot step. Example: a 10,000 USD account risking 1% (100 USD) on EURUSD with a 20-pip stop. One lot is 100,000 EUR, so a 0.0020 move costs 200 USD per lot, and 100 ÷ 200 = 0.50 lots. With USDJPY at 150.00 the pip value is 100,000 × 0.01 ÷ 150 = 6.67 USD per lot, a 20-pip stop costs 133.33 USD per lot, and the size is 0.75 lots.

What is the difference between a pip and a point?

For currency pairs a pip is the fourth decimal (0.0001), or the second for pairs quoted in JPY or HUF (0.01). For indices and crypto this page uses points of 1.0. MetaTrader's "points" are different again: a point there is the smallest price step, which on a 5-digit EURUSD quote is 0.00001, a tenth of a pip. If your platform shows a stop of 200 points on EURUSD, that is 20 pips.

Why is the size rounded down, not to the nearest step?

Brokers accept sizes only in steps, usually 0.01 lots. Rounding up would put more than your chosen risk on the trade; rounding down never does. The calculator shows the money at risk at the rounded size and warns when rounding cuts the risk by more than 10%, or when even the minimum lot would risk more than you chose. Small accounts with wide stops hit this often.

Why do index and crypto contract sizes vary by broker?

Index and crypto CFDs are defined by each broker. One lot of US30 can be 1, 10 or 0.1 times the index; one lot of BTCUSD can be one coin or a fraction of one. Forex (100,000 units) and gold (100 oz) are close to universal, though not guaranteed. Check "Contract size" in your platform's symbol specification and enter it under Broker settings; the result changes with it.

Which exchange rate is used?

The European Central Bank's reference rates, published once a working day around 16:00 CET, through the free frankfurter.dev service. Your broker converts at the live rate, so the money at risk can differ slightly, more on volatile days. For an exact number, type the live rate into the exchange rate field; it is written the way your platform quotes it (for example 1 USD = 150 JPY).

Does it include spread and commission?

No. The risk is the loss between entry and stop. Spread, commission and slippage at the stop add to it, so a trade with a tight stop costs more than the number shown. Edge Check shows what costs do to a trade record.
